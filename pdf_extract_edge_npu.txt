Intelligent Edge Application Deployment for NPU: A Comparative
Framework Across CNN, LLM, and VLA Architectures on
Embedded Hardware
Siddharth Bejadi
School of Computing and Information Science
ARU Cambridge, United Kingdom
Abstract—Deploying AI models on edge neural processing
units (NPUs) demands training strategies for hardware con-
straints, but we have no framework or techniques to develop
an IEA (Intelligent Edge Application). This paper proposes
a systematic framework classifying training approaches for
edge NPU deployment into three levels: software-only (post-
training quantization), hybrid (quantization-aware training and
knowledge distillation) and hardware-aware (hardware-aware
neural architecture search and compiler-in-the-loop co-design).
I have analyzed the feasibility and trade-offs of each approach
across three architecture families—convolutional neural networks
(CNNs), large language models (LLMs), and vision-language-
action (VLA) models—Mapping which model architectures suit
which deployment approaches, based on current toolchain sup-
port and published benchmarks. The framework is validated
through a case study deploying YOLOv8n on the STM32N6570-
DK development board with the Neural-ART NPU, achieving
29 ms inference at INT8 post-training quantization and doc-
umenting how training strategy can directly impact memory
footprint, inference, deploying debugging effort. My analysis
reveals that CNNs are well-served by all three training levels,
LLMs remain largely restricted to software-only approaches
and impractical on microcontroller-class NPUs due to memory
bandwidth constraint, and VLAs represent a research frontier
where only the most aggressive architectural compression enables
edge feasibility. I conclude with a six-step decision framework and
identify unsolved challenges toward fully cloud-independent AI
systems.

I. INTRODUCTION
The deployment of artificial intelligence on edge devices
has shifted from an aspirational goal to an engineering imper-
ative. Applications spanning autonomous robotics, industrial
inspection, wearable health monitoring, and smart infras-
tructure increasingly demand inference at the point of data
generation—driven by latency requirements below 100 mil-
liseconds, privacy constraints that prohibit cloud transmission,
connectivity limitations in remote or safety-critical environ-
ments, and energy budgets measured in milliwatts [1], [2].
This shift has catalysed a proliferation of Neural Processing
Units (NPUs) embedded within microcontroller-class devices,
offering dedicated hardware acceleration for deep learning
inference under strict power and memory constraints [3], [4].
However, the dominant focus of edge AI research has
been on inference optimisation—techniques appliedaftera
model has been trained, such as post-training quantization,
pruning, and knowledge distillation [5], [6]. Comparatively
little attention has been paid to how the training process itself
should be adapted when the deployment target is an edge NPU
rather than a cloud GPU. This gap is consequential: the choice
of training strategy has cascading effects on accuracy preser-
vation, deployment debugging effort, toolchain compatibility,
and ultimately whether a model can be deployed at all.
The challenge is compounded by the diversity of model
architectures now being considered for edge deployment.
Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) represent the most
mature edge AI workload, with well-characterised quantiza-
tion behaviour and comprehensive NPU toolchain support
[7]. Large language models (LLMs), driven by the emer-
gence of small language model families such as Phi-3 [8],
Llama 3.2 [9], and MobileLLM [10], are rapidly moving
toward on-device deployment but face fundamental memory
bandwidth bottlenecks on microcontroller-class hardware [11],
[12]. Vision-language-action (VLA) models, which combine
visual perception with language understanding and robotic
control, represent the newest frontier—with SmolVLA at 450
million parameters [13] and EdgeVLA [14] demonstrating that
compact VLA architectures can approach the performance
of models ten times larger, though MCU-class deployment
remains infeasible.
Each architecture family constrains the feasible training-to-
deployment pipeline differently. A training strategy that works
for deploying YOLOv8 on an STM32N6 NPU may be entirely
inappropriate for deploying a quantized LLM on a Qualcomm
Hexagon NPU or a VLA on NVIDIA Jetson. Yet no unified
framework exists to guide practitioners through this decision
space.
This paper addresses this gap with four contributions:
1) A taxonomy of training approaches for edge NPU
deployment, classifying strategies into three lev-
els: software-only post-training quantization, hybrid
quantization-aware training, and hardware-aware co-
design.
2) A comparative analysis across CNN, LLM, and VLA ar-
chitectures, establishing an architecture-approach com-
patibility matrix.
3) Experimental validation through a case study deploying
YOLOv8n on the STM32N6570-DK with the Neural-

4) A practical six-step decision framework enabling practi-

II. BACKGROUND ANDRELATEDWORK
A. Edge AI Architectures
Edge AI encompasses the deployment of artificial intelli-
gence algorithms directly on devices at the network periphery,
shifting computation from centralised cloud data centres to
distributed edge nodes with significantly constrained resources
[1]. Most ultra-low-power edge systems operate below approx-
imately 200 mW, rising to 1–10 W for MCU-class processors
with dedicated NPUs [3].
CNNsrepresent the most mature edge AI workload. The
YOLO family, MobileNet variants, and EfficientNet-Lite have
been progressively optimised for edge deployment through
depthwise separable convolutions, inverted residual blocks,
and squeeze-and-excitation modules [7]. These architectures
produce regular compute patterns that map efficiently to NPU
multiply-accumulate (MAC) arrays. INT8 post-training quan-
tization on well-designed CNNs typically incurs less than 1%
accuracy loss on standard benchmarks [15], making them the
default choice for NPU-targeted perception tasks.
LLMs at the edgerepresent an emerging but severely
constrained deployment target. Meta’s Llama 3.2 offers 1B
and 3B variants optimised for edge devices [9]; Microsoft’s
Phi-3 achieves performance comparable to much larger models
through data-centric training [8]; and MobileLLM targets sub-
billion parameter models through architecture search [10].
However, even the smallest practical LLMs require hundreds
of megabytes of weight storage at INT4 quantization—two
orders of magnitude beyond MCU-class SRAM capacities.
Recent benchmarks confirm that LLM token generation on
edge hardware is fundamentally memory-bandwidth-bound
rather than compute-bound [12], [16]. Qualcomm’s NPU doc-
umentation states explicitly that memory bandwidth is the
bottleneck for LLM token generation rather than processing
capacity [16].
VLA modelsare the newest class, integrating visual per-
ception, language understanding, and robotic control within
unified architectures [13], [14]. SmolVLA (450M parameters)
demonstrated that compact VLAs can match models ten times
larger on simulation benchmarks while being trainable on a
single GPU [13]. EdgeVLA eliminated autoregressive action
prediction for a seven-fold inference speedup [14]. LiteVLA
achieved the first CPU-only VLA deployment on a Raspberry
Pi 4 using GGUF quantization and LoRA fine-tuning [17]. De-
spite these advances, VLA edge deployment remains restricted
to Jetson-class or mobile SoC platforms.
B. Neural Processing Units
NPUs are domain-specific accelerators designed to max-
imise operations per watt for neural network inference [3],
[4]. The STM32N6 Neural-ART NPU pairs an ARM Cortex-
M55 core with a dedicated neural accelerator delivering 600
GOPS at INT8, drawing 0.8–1.2 W under active inference [3],
[18]. Capogrosso et al. benchmarked this platform at 13.7 ms
latency and 29.5 MAC/cycle on PicoSAM2, with an energy-
delay product (EDP) of 206.8 mJ·s [3]. The GreenWaves
GAP9 provides a RISC-V cluster at 64 mW achieving 182.15
MMAC/J [3]. The Sony IMX500 achieves 86.2 MAC/cycle
and the lowest EDP at 3.4 mJ·s through in-sensor compute
[3].
Mobile SoC NPUs (Qualcomm Hexagon, Apple Neural
Engine, Google Edge TPU) offer 10–45 TOPS with 6–16 GB
shared LPDDR [12], [16]. Critical constraints include limited
on-chip SRAM (0.5–4 MB for MCU-class), restricted operator
sets, fixed-point arithmetic requirements, and memory band-
width limitations.
C. Model Compression and Quantization
Post-Training Quantization (PTQ)converts a pre-trained
FP32 model to lower-precision formats without retraining
[5]. Key methods include GPTQ for layer-wise second-order
optimisation [19], AWQ for activation-aware weight quanti-
zation [20], and SmoothQuant for hardware-friendly W8A8
quantization [21]. PTQ’s advantage is speed; its limitation is
accuracy degradation below INT8.
Quantization-Aware Training (QAT)inserts fake quanti-
zation nodes into the training graph, allowing models to learn
weight distributions robust to quantization noise [15]. QAT
narrows the accuracy gap to less than 0.5% at INT8, and
recent work demonstrates 4–22% accuracy recovery over PTQ
at INT4 on LLM reasoning benchmarks [22].
Hardware-Aware NASco-designs neural architectures
with inference engines. MCUNet achieved 70.7% ImageNet
accuracy on STM32H743 with 320 kB SRAM [7]. Once-for-
All trains a supernet for rapid hardware-specific specialisation
[23]. NanoNAS demonstrated laptop-scale HW-NAS targeting
sub-512 KB microcontrollers [24].
Additional techniques includeknowledge distillation(10–
50×size reduction with 90–95% accuracy retention [25]) and
LoRAfor parameter-efficient fine-tuning [26].
III. PROPOSEDFRAMEWORK: TRAININGAPPROACHES
FOREDGEDEPLOYMENT
A. Taxonomy of Training Approaches
We propose a three-level taxonomy organised by the degree
of hardware awareness integrated into the training process.
Level 1—Software-Only (Post-Hoc Optimisation).The
standard pipeline: train on cloud GPUs using FP32/FP16,
export, apply PTQ, convert via the NPU toolchain, and deploy.
This treats model and hardware as independent concerns.
Advantages include minimal complexity, no retraining, broad
toolchain support (TFLite, ONNX, ST Edge AI Core, Open-
VINO), and fast iteration (minutes to hours). Limitations
include accuracy degradation at low bit-widths and high de-
ployment debugging burden, since quantization issues surface
only at the deployment stage.
Level 2—Hybrid (Training-Time Awareness).The train-
ing process incorporates awareness of target precision but

C. Decision Framework
The proposed six-step decision framework proceeds as
follows:
Step 1: Model family identification.CNN, LLM, or
VLA—immediately constraining viable approaches per Ta-
ble I.
Step 2: NPU constraint mapping.Profile available
SRAM/Flash, supported operators, quantization formats, band-
width, and power budget.
Step 3: Toolchain compatibility check.Verify that the
intended training output format is accepted. ST Edge AI
Core accepts TFLite INT8 and ONNX INT8, constraining the
pipeline upstream.
Step 4: Accuracy requirement assessment.Less than
1% loss acceptable at INT8 for a well-characterised CNN
suggests PTQ sufficiency. Tighter requirements or transformer
architectures recommend QAT.
Step 5: Engineering budget evaluation.PTQ requires
hours; QAT requires days; HW-NAS requires weeks. If de-

IV. COMPARATIVEANALYSIS OFTRAININGAPPROACHES
A. Accuracy Preservation
For CNNs at INT8, PTQ yields less than 1% accuracy
loss on ImageNet [15]. QAT reduces this to less than 0.5%,
with the gap widening at lower precision—at INT4, PTQ
can lose 3–8% while QAT recovers most through learned
compensation [5], [22]. HW-NAS achieves highest absolute
accuracy within resource envelopes: MCUNet’s 70.7% on
hardware where PTQ-compressed standard architectures man-
aged approximately 65% [7].
For LLMs, PTQ via GPTQ/AWQ at INT4 is dominant.
Reasoning tasks show higher sensitivity: NVIDIA benchmarks
demonstrate 4–22% QAT accuracy recovery on mathematical
reasoning at 4-bit [22]. At INT8, the PTQ-QAT gap narrows,
making PTQ the practical default.

VLA accuracy data remains limited. SmolVLA achieves
87% success on LIBERO benchmarks with standard training
followed by quantization [13]. No systematic QAT study for
VLAs has been published.
B. Engineering Complexity and Iteration Speed
PTQ offers the lowest barrier—a trained model can be quan-
tized in minutes using TFLite, ONNX, or GPTQ. No training
pipeline modification is required. However, deployment-stage
debugging of quantization artifacts can consume days.
QAT requires training loop modification (fake quantization
nodes), adding 10–20% additional epochs. Critically, QAT
implementation must match the target toolchain’s scheme—
using PyTorch QAT for an ST Edge AI Core target (TFLite
INT8) requires additional ONNX conversion that may negate
accuracy benefits.
HW-NAS imposes the highest overhead. MCUNet’s search
requires hardware profiling, constrained search space defini-
tion, architecture search (200+ GPU-hours), and training from
scratch [7]. NanoNAS reduced this to laptop-scale [24], but
architectures remain hardware-specific.
C. Hardware Generalisability and Toolchain Dependency
Software-only approaches produce the most portable
models—the same FP32 model can be quantized differently
for different NPUs. Hybrid approaches are moderately portable
across same-precision NPUs. Hardware-aware approaches pro-
duce the least portable models, tightly bound to specific
hardware targets.
The NPU toolchain acts as a hard upstream constraint.
ST Edge AI Core accepts TFLite INT8 and ONNX INT8
[18]; TFLite for Edge TPU requires full integer quantization;
TensorRT for Jetson supports FP16, INT8, and INT4. Three
independent papers identify toolchain immaturity as a field-
wide problem [3], [6], [11], confirming that platform fragmen-
tation is the largest barrier to standardised training-for-edge
pipelines.
V. CASESTUDY: YOLOV8N ONSTM32N6570-DK
A. Experimental Setup
The case study deploys YOLOv8n (Ultralytics) for cus-
tom object detection (car keys and house keys) on the
STM32N6570-DK. The target hardware features the Neural-
ART NPU at 600 GOPS (INT8), ARM Cortex-M55,
1.5 MB on-chip SRAM, and external flash via XSPI2. The
toolchain comprises ST Edge AI Core (stedgeai v4.0.0),
STM32CubeIDE, and STM32CubeProgrammer. The training
approach was Level 1 (software-only): standard GPU training
via Ultralytics followed by TFLite INT8 PTQ.
B. Deployment Pipeline
The deployment followed five stages: (1) train YOLOv8n
using Ultralytics with standard FP32 training; (2) export to
TFLite with INT8 quantization using a representative cali-
bration dataset; (3) convert via ST Edge AI Core for the
Neural-ART NPU; (4) flash NPU weights to external flash
at specified addresses; (5) configure firmware for memory-
mapped inference via XSPI2.
C. Results
The deployment achieved29 ms inference per frame, down
from approximately 14 seconds in initial non-NPU (CPU-only)
attempts—a 483×speedup. This is consistent with Capogrosso
et al.’s 13.7 ms for PicoSAM2 on the same NPU [3], with the
difference attributable to YOLOv8n’s larger architecture. At
29 ms, the system achieves approximately 34 FPS, meeting the
10–30 Hz decision rates for real-time robotics [6] and falling
below the 100 ms interactive threshold.
D. Deployment Challenges and Training Strategy Implications
Four categories of deployment challenges were encountered:
INT8 tensor shape mismatches:The Ultralytics TFLite
export produced tensor dimensions the ST Edge AI Core con-
verter did not expect, requiring iterative cross-tool debugging.
QAT would have mitigated this by validating against the target
quantization format during training.
DCache coherency issues:The NPU’s direct memory
access conflicted with the Cortex-M55’s data cache, causing
intermittent incorrect results. This is a hardware-level issue no
training strategy prevents.
NPU weight flash address corrections:Quantized weights
required specific memory addresses for memory-mapped ac-
cess. Misalignment caused silent failures with limited diag-
nostic information—consistent with the toolchain immaturity
identified across all three surveyed papers [3], [6], [11].
XSPI2 configuration:Manual firmware modification was
required for the external flash memory-mapped mode. This is
a system-level issue independent of training strategy.
Of four challenge categories, QAT would have directly
mitigated two (tensor mismatches and weight format issues).
The practical calculus: if deployment debugging exceeds two
to three times the QAT fine-tuning cost, QAT is the more
efficient path.
E. Training Approach Justification
Level 1 was selected because YOLOv8n is a well-
characterised CNN, the Neural-ART NPU is CNN-optimised
with mature operator support, and the deployment was a
research prototype prioritising iteration speed. Level 2 was
unnecessary given acceptable accuracy but would have been
recommended had debugging time exceeded QAT cost. Level 3
was unnecessary since YOLOv8n already fits within the NPU’s
resource envelope.
VI. DISCUSSION
A. Key Insights
The most significant finding is that the training approach
shapes the entire deployment experience, not merely accu-
racy. PTQ’s simplicity at training is offset by unpredictable
deployment debugging, while QAT’s upfront complexity yields
smoother deployment—mirroring the software engineering

The compatibility matrix reveals a stark maturity gradient.
CNNs benefit from two decades of co-evolution between
model design, compression, and hardware. LLMs are ap-

quantization as the practical ceiling on MCU-class hardware.
VLAs trail by another one to two years, with edge deployment
achievable only through aggressive architectural innovation
rather than standardised compression.
B. The Memory Bandwidth Wall
Memory bandwidth—not compute (TOPS)—is the binding
constraint for on-device LLMs. The STM32N6 offers 600
GOPS, theoretically sufficient for small transformers. How-
ever, its external flash interface provides bandwidth orders of
magnitude below autoregressive demands. Each token requires
full weight matrix loading. This is confirmed by Intel’s NPU
documentation: “although NPU performance still matters, it
becomes less of the bottleneck compared to the available
DRAM bandwidth” [16]. This constraint requires hardware-
level solutions—compute-in-memory, 3D-stacked SRAM, or
hybrid execution—rather than better training strategies.
C. VLAs: The Emerging Frontier
SmolVLA’s dual-system architecture (frozen VLM
backbone + lightweight action expert), EdgeVLA’s non-
autoregressive prediction, and LiteVLA’s GGUF+LoRA
approach collectively demonstrate that VLA edge deployment
will follow the same trajectory as LLM deployment—driven
by architectural innovation rather than compression of large
models [13], [14], [17]. The lessons from CNN pipelines
(co-design model and engine, prioritise memory scheduling,
prefer structured compression) are directly transferable. Fig. 4
visualises this maturity gradient across all three architecture
families.
D. Limitations
The experimental validation covers only one architecture
(CNN) on one NPU (STM32N6). Claims regarding LLMs and
VLAs are grounded in published literature rather than first-
hand deployment. The rapidly evolving toolchain landscape
may outdate specific compatibility claims. The framework
assumes single-model deployment and does not address multi-
model orchestration.
VII. CONCLUSION ANDFUTUREDIRECTIONS
This paper has presented a systematic framework for select-
ing training approaches when deploying AI models on edge
NPUs. The three-level taxonomy—software-only PTQ, hybrid

--- PAGE 7 ---
QAT, and hardware-aware NAS—provides a structured vocab-
ulary for the training-deployment interface. The architecture-
approach compatibility matrix establishes that CNNs are well-
served across all levels, LLMs are constrained to software-
only on MCU-class hardware, and VLAs require aggressive
architectural innovation for edge feasibility.
The YOLOv8n case study validates that PTQ is sufficient
for well-characterised CNNs on mature NPU platforms, but
demonstrates that training strategy choice materially impacts
deployment debugging. Two of four debugging categories
would have been mitigated by QAT, suggesting the total cost
of PTQ (training + debugging) can exceed QAT (training +
fine-tuning) even when PTQ accuracy is acceptable.
Unsolved challenges toward fully cloud-independent AI
include: on-device training remains impractical for MCU-
class hardware [7]; no standardised training-to-edge pipeline
exists across NPU vendors [11]; the memory bandwidth wall
prevents LLM deployment on MCUs; unified benchmarking
is absent [3]; energy reporting lacks consistency [11]; multi-
model scheduling has no formal guarantees [6]; and continual
on-device learning remains an open problem.
Future work should extend this framework with case studies
deploying quantized LLMs on RISC-V NPUs and compact
VLAs on Jetson platforms to validate the compatibility matrix
with first-hand deployment data across all three architecture
families.
